\section{Tensors, restrictions, and arithmetic algorithms}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

This section fixes conventions and proves, in the form used later, the
standard tools that turn tensor identities into algorithms: identities
among matrix multiplication, dot-product, and convolution tensors;
composition and exactification of degenerations; and the pooled recursion,
which turns a relative scheme \(r_*\MM_A\restr m_*\MM_B\) into an algorithm
with exponent \(\log(r_*/m_*)/\log(B/A)\) (Lemma~\ref{lem:batch}).

\subsection{Tensors, restrictions, and leg permutations}
\label{subsec:prelim-conventions}

All logarithms are natural, and all vector spaces are finite dimensional.
We work over \(\C\); the coefficients of the main construction lie in a
cyclotomic field (Theorem~\ref{thm:effective}), and those of its
rational variant in \(\Q\) (Section~\ref{subsec:rational-variant}). For a positive integer
\(m\), \(\zeta_m\) denotes a primitive \(m\)-th root of unity.

A \emph{tensor} is a trilinear form in three disjoint groups of variables,
called its \emph{legs}. We name the variables of legs \(1\), \(2\), and
\(3\) with the letters \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\), respectively. The rank
\(\R(T)\) of a tensor \(T\) is the least number of products of three linear
forms, one in the variables of each leg, whose sum is \(T\). The
\emph{flattening} of \(T\) along a leg is its coefficient matrix with rows
indexed by the variables of that leg and columns indexed by pairs of
variables of the other two legs; its rank is at most \(\R(T)\).

We write \(T\restr T'\), and call \(T'\) a \emph{restriction} of \(T\), if
linear substitutions on each leg transform \(T\) into \(T'\): every variable
of \(T\) is replaced by a linear form in the variables of the same leg of
\(T'\). We read a tensor as a bilinear map: its two arguments are the values
of the variables of legs \(1\) and \(2\), and its result is the vector of
coefficients of the variables of leg \(3\). In this reading, \(T\restr T'\)
says that \(T'\) can be evaluated by applying linear maps to its two
arguments, evaluating \(T\), and applying a linear map to the result. In
particular \(\R(T')\le\R(T)\), and no flattening rank increases under
restriction. We write \(T\cong T'\), and call the tensors
\emph{isomorphic}, if \(T'\) arises from \(T\) by a bijection from the
variables of each leg of \(T\) to the variables of the same leg of \(T'\);
isomorphic tensors are restrictions of each other.

For a positive integer \(m\), the tensor \(mT\) is the full direct sum of
\(m\) copies of \(T\), with independent variables on all three legs;
\(\oplus\) denotes full direct sums in general. Juxtaposition denotes the
tensor product, whose leg-\(i\) variables are the products of the leg-\(i\)
variables of the factors, and \(T^{\otimes n}\) is a tensor power. Tensor
products distribute over direct sums, so that \((mT)T'\cong m(TT')\), and
reordering the factors of a tensor product is an isomorphism.

For \(\pi\in S_3\), the tensor \(T^\pi\) places leg \(i\) of \(T\) at
position \(\pi(i)\), so that \((T^{\pi'})^{\pi}=T^{\pi\pi'}\). In a product
such as \(T^\pi T'\), leg \(i\) of \(T\) lies
in leg \(\pi(i)\) of the product; we call the legs of the product its
\emph{global} legs. Let \(\mathcal A_+\) and \(\mathcal A_-\) be the sets of
even and odd permutations, and write \((23)\) for the transposition of legs
\(2\) and \(3\). Every identity of this paper also holds under the opposite
convention (\(T^\pi\mapsto T^{\pi^{-1}}\), \(\pi\pi'\mapsto\pi'\pi\)):
permutations enter only through the sets \(\mathcal A_\pm\), which are
closed under inversion, and \(\pi\pi'\) and \(\pi'\pi\) lie in the same set
because \(\mathcal A_+\) is normal in \(S_3\).

\subsection{Matrix multiplication, dot-product, and convolution tensors}
\label{subsec:prelim-tensors}

The matrix multiplication tensor of side \(d\) is
\(\MM_d=\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma=0}^{d-1}
x_{\alpha\beta}\,y_{\beta\gamma}\,z_{\gamma\alpha}\). As a bilinear map it
multiplies the matrices \((x_{\alpha\beta})\) and
\((y_{\beta\gamma})\): the coefficient of \(z_{\gamma\alpha}\) is the entry
\((\alpha,\gamma)\) of their product. The \emph{dot-product tensors}
\(B_X(d)=x\sum_{\gamma=0}^{d-1}y_\gamma z_\gamma\),
\(B_Y(d)=y\sum_{\gamma=0}^{d-1}x_\gamma z_\gamma\), and
\(B_Z(d)=z\sum_{\gamma=0}^{d-1}x_\gamma y_\gamma\) each have one leg with a
single variable. Relabeling variables gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix-products}
 \MM_d\MM_{d'}\cong\MM_{dd'},\qquad
 B_X(d)B_Y(d)B_Z(d)\cong\MM_d .
\end{equation}
For the first, merge corresponding indices of the two factors. For the
second, call the summation indices of \(B_X(d)\), \(B_Y(d)\), and
\(B_Z(d)\) \(\gamma\), \(\alpha\), and \(\beta\); then the leg-\(1\),
leg-\(2\), and leg-\(3\) variables of the product are determined by
\((\alpha,\beta)\), \((\beta,\gamma)\), and \((\gamma,\alpha)\), and
renaming them gives \(\MM_d\).

The tensor \(B_X(d)^\pi\) has its single-variable leg at position
\(\pi(1)\), and \(B_X(d)^{(23)}\cong B_X(d)\); hence \(B_X(d)^\pi\) is
isomorphic to \(B_X(d)\), \(B_Y(d)\), or \(B_Z(d)\) according as
\(\pi(1)\) is \(1\), \(2\), or \(3\). In each of
\(\mathcal A_+=\{\mathrm{id},(123),(132)\}\) and
\(\mathcal A_-=\{(12),(13),(23)\}\), the value \(\pi(1)\) runs through
\(1,2,3\) once. By \eqref{eq:matrix-products}, therefore,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coset-dot-products}
 \bigotimes_{\pi\in\mathcal A_\sigma}B_X(d)^\pi\cong\MM_d
 \quad(\sigma\in\{+,-\}),\qquad
 \bigotimes_{\pi\in S_3}B_X(d)^\pi\cong\MM_{d^2}.
\end{equation}
The construction uses the first identity, for the three orientations of one
set \(\mathcal A_\sigma\); the second, for all six orientations, serves only
the exact binary certificate of Appendix~\ref{sec:exact-certificate}.

For positive integers \(r\) and \(b\), the \emph{convolution tensor} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convolution}
 C(r,b)=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{r-1}\sum_{\beta=0}^{b-1}
        x_\alpha\, y_\beta\, z_{\alpha+\beta}.
\end{equation}
Its legs have \(r\), \(b\), and \(r+b-1\) variables. If \(x_\alpha\) and
\(y_\beta\) are the coefficients of two polynomials with \(r\) and \(b\)
coefficients, the bilinear map \(C(r,b)\) returns the \(r+b-1\) coefficients
of their product. For \(r=1\), the tensor \(C(1,b)\) is \(B_X(b)\).

The rank of \(C(r,b)\) is \(r+b-1\). To see this, put
\(\zeta=\zeta_{r+b-1}\). Evaluation at the points \(\zeta^\ell\),
followed by interpolation, gives the rank decomposition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convolution-rank}
 C(r,b)=\frac1{r+b-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{r+b-2}
 \Bigl(\sum_{\alpha=0}^{r-1}\zeta^{\ell\alpha}x_\alpha\Bigr)
 \Bigl(\sum_{\beta=0}^{b-1}\zeta^{\ell\beta}y_\beta\Bigr)
 \Bigl(\sum_{\gamma=0}^{r+b-2}\zeta^{-\ell\gamma}z_\gamma\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Indeed, the coefficient of \(x_\alpha y_\beta z_\gamma\) on the right is
\(1\) if \(\alpha+\beta\equiv\gamma\pmod{r+b-1}\) and \(0\) otherwise,
and since \(\alpha+\beta\) and \(\gamma\) lie in \(\{0,\ldots,r+b-2\}\),
the congruence forces \(\gamma=\alpha+\beta\). Conversely, the flattening
of \(C(r,b)\) along leg \(3\) has rank \(r+b-1\), because the rows of the
variables \(z_\gamma\) are nonzero and have disjoint supports. The tensors
\(B_X(d)\) and \(C(r,b)\), the second identity in
\eqref{eq:matrix-products}, and the rank of \(C(r,b)\) also appear
in \citet[Sections~2 and~4]{OAI26}.

Since Section~\ref{sec:maps} applies each relation simultaneously to the
three oriented copies \(C(r,b)^\pi\), \(\pi\in\mathcal A_\sigma\), the
construction tracks the \emph{symmetrized convolution tensors}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclic-def}
 S^\sigma_{r,b}=\bigotimes_{\pi\in\mathcal A_\sigma}C(r,b)^\pi,
 \qquad \sigma\in\{+,-\}.
\end{equation}
We call \(\sigma\) the \emph{sign} of \(S^\sigma_{r,b}\), and we regard
\(S^\sigma_{r,b}\) as a product of three factors indexed by
\(\pi\in\mathcal A_\sigma\), also when some of these factors are
isomorphic. These tensors satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cyclic-identities}
 S^\sigma_{r,b}\cong S^{-\sigma}_{b,r},\qquad
 S^+_{r,r}\cong S^-_{r,r},\qquad
 S^\sigma_{1,b}\cong\MM_b,
 \qquad \R(S^\sigma_{r,b})\le(r+b-1)^3.
\end{equation}
For the first identity, exchanging the first two legs of \(C(r,b)\) gives
\(C(b,r)\), that is, \(C(b,r)=C(r,b)^{(12)}\). Hence
\(S^{-\sigma}_{b,r}
=\bigotimes_{\pi\in\mathcal A_{-\sigma}}\bigl(C(r,b)^{(12)}\bigr)^\pi
=\bigotimes_{\pi\in\mathcal A_{-\sigma}}C(r,b)^{\pi(12)}\). As \(\pi\)
runs through \(\mathcal A_{-\sigma}\), the product \(\pi(12)\), which has
the opposite parity, runs through \(\mathcal A_\sigma\), so both sides have
the same three factors in a different order. Taking \(b=r\) gives the second identity.
For the third, \(C(1,b)=B_X(b)\), so \(S^\sigma_{1,b}\cong\MM_b\) by
\eqref{eq:coset-dot-products}. The rank bound follows by tensoring three
copies of \eqref{eq:convolution-rank}, since permuting legs does not change
rank. For \(r,b\ge2\) with \(r\ne b\), these identities do not relate
\(S^+_{r,b}\) to \(S^-_{r,b}\); Section~\ref{subsec:orientations} explains
why the construction needs no such relation.

Appendix~\ref{sec:exact-certificate}, which compares the construction with
the exact binary certificate, uses the sixfold product \(Q_{r,b}\) below;
its properties follow from \eqref{eq:cyclic-identities}
and \eqref{eq:matrix-products}:
\begin{gather}
 Q_{r,b}=S^+_{r,b}S^-_{r,b}
        =\textstyle\bigotimes_{\pi\in S_3}C(r,b)^\pi ,
 \label{eq:balanced}\\
 Q_{r,b}\cong Q_{b,r},\qquad
 \R(Q_{r,b})\le(r+b-1)^6,\qquad Q_{1,2}\cong\MM_2\MM_2\cong\MM_4 .
 \label{eq:balanced-properties}
\end{gather}

\subsection{Degenerations and exactification}
\label{subsec:prelim-degenerations}

The local relations of Section~\ref{sec:maps} are degenerations: their maps
depend on a parameter \(t\), and they produce the desired tensor only as the
constant term of a polynomial in \(t\). The letter \(t\) denotes this
parameter; as a subscript, as in \(M_{\mathrm t}\) and \(\mathcal I_t\), it
abbreviates ``tripling''.

Let \(T\) and \(T'\) be tensors and let \(D\ge0\) be an integer. We write
\(T\degen T'\), and call this a \emph{degeneration of degree at most
\(D\)}, if a triple \(\Psi(t)\) of linear maps, one on each leg, whose
matrix entries are Laurent polynomials in \(t\), transforms \(T\) into a
polynomial in \(t\) of degree at most \(D\) with tensor coefficients and
constant term \(T'\); the entries of \(\Psi(t)\) may contain negative
powers of \(t\), but the transformed tensor may not. Degenerations enter the
construction only through the next two lemmas. Each local degeneration
(Corollary~\ref{cor:oriented-relations}) is a type restriction, then a
separator, then a reindexing, and Section~\ref{sec:extraction} tensors all
of them with one parameter; Lemma~\ref{lem:composition} shows that maps independent of
\(t\), applied before or after a degeneration, keep its degree bound, and
that degree bounds add under tensor products.

\begin{lemma}[Composition]\label{lem:composition}
For each \(i\) in a finite index set, let \(\mathcal U_i\) be a tensor, let
\(\mathsf T_i\) and \(\mathsf R_i\) be triples of linear maps that do not
depend on \(t\), and let \(\mathsf S_i(t)\) be a triple of linear maps with
Laurent-polynomial entries. Suppose that \(\mathsf S_i(t)\) transforms
\(\mathsf T_i(\mathcal U_i)\) into a polynomial \(\widetilde T_i(t)\) in
\(t\) of degree at most \(D_i\) with constant term \(\widetilde T_{i,0}\),
and that \(\mathsf R_i\) is defined on all target variables of
\(\mathsf S_i(t)\). Then the composite
\(\mathsf R_i\circ\mathsf S_i(t)\circ\mathsf T_i\) transforms
\(\mathcal U_i\) into the polynomial obtained by applying \(\mathsf R_i\) to
each coefficient of \(\widetilde T_i(t)\), so
\(\mathcal U_i\degen\mathsf R_i(\widetilde T_{i,0})\) with degree at most
\(D_i\). Applying these composites to the factors of
\(\bigotimes_i\mathcal U_i\), with one common parameter \(t\), gives a
polynomial in \(t\) of degree at most \(\sum_iD_i\) with constant term
\(\bigotimes_i\mathsf R_i(\widetilde T_{i,0})\).

For \(\pi\in S_3\), let \(\mathsf T_i^\pi\), \(\mathsf S_i(t)^\pi\), and
\(\mathsf R_i^\pi\) assign to leg \(\pi(l)\) the maps that \(\mathsf T_i\),
\(\mathsf S_i(t)\), and \(\mathsf R_i\) assign to leg \(l\). Then
\(\mathsf S_i(t)^\pi\circ\mathsf T_i^\pi\) transforms
\(\mathcal U_i^\pi\) into \(\widetilde T_i(t)^\pi\), on which
\(\mathsf R_i^\pi\) acts coefficient by coefficient as \(\mathsf R_i\)
acts on \(\widetilde T_i(t)\). Hence both statements also hold with each
\(\mathcal U_i\) replaced by \(\mathcal U_i^{\pi_i}\), for every choice of
\(\pi_i\in S_3\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since \(\mathsf R_i\) is linear and independent of \(t\), it acts
coefficient by coefficient. In the expanded tensor product the exponents of
\(t\) add and are nonnegative, so the degree is at most \(\sum_iD_i\), and
a term has exponent zero exactly when every factor contributes its constant
term. Permuting legs reassigns the maps of each triple to other legs and
changes no coefficient and no power of \(t\).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Exactification~\citep{Bini80}]\label{lem:exactification}
Suppose that a triple \(\Psi(t)\) of linear maps with Laurent-polynomial
entries transforms a tensor \(T\) into a polynomial \(\widetilde T(t)\) in
\(t\) of degree at most \(D\) with constant term \(\widetilde T_0\). Then,
for every positive integer \(k\),
\[
 (kD+1)\,T^{\otimes k}\restr\widetilde T_0^{\otimes k}.
\]
With \(E=kD+1\), this restriction uses the maps
\(\Psi(\zeta_E^\ell)^{\otimes k}\), \(0\le\ell<E\), and the scalar \(1/E\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put \(E=kD+1\) and \(\zeta=\zeta_E\). Since \(\zeta^\ell\ne0\), the
Laurent entries of \(\Psi\) can be evaluated at \(\zeta^\ell\), and
\(\Psi(\zeta^\ell)^{\otimes k}\) transforms \(T^{\otimes k}\) into
\(\widetilde T(\zeta^\ell)^{\otimes k}\). As \(\widetilde T(t)^{\otimes k}\)
is a polynomial in \(t\) of degree at most \(kD=E-1\) with constant term
\(\widetilde T_0^{\otimes k}\), and
\(\sum_{\ell=0}^{E-1}\zeta^{\ell\eta}=0\) for \(0<\eta<E\),
\[
 \frac1E\sum_{\ell=0}^{E-1}\widetilde T(\zeta^\ell)^{\otimes k}
 =\widetilde T_0^{\otimes k}.
\]
Apply the \(\ell\)-th triple to the \(\ell\)-th summand of
\(E\,T^{\otimes k}\), send the copy of each target variable in every
summand to that variable, which turns the direct sum of the \(E\) images
into their sum, and multiply the map on one leg by \(1/E\). All these maps
are linear on each leg, so \(E\,T^{\otimes k}\restr\widetilde
T_0^{\otimes k}\).
\end{proof}

\subsection{From relative schemes to algorithms}
\label{subsec:relative-schemes}

We measure algorithms by their arithmetic cost: the number of additions,
subtractions, and multiplications of field elements, including
multiplications by fixed coefficients. An algorithm may depend on a fixed
exponent allowance \(\eps\) but not on the input dimension \(n\); its fixed
coefficients are computed once, in a preprocessing step whose cost depends
on \(\eps\) only.

\begin{definition}[Relative scheme]\label{def:relative-scheme}
A \emph{relative scheme} is a restriction
\(r_*\MM_A\restr m_*\MM_B\), given by its three linear maps, where
\(r_*\), \(m_*\), \(A\), and \(B\) are positive integers with \(B>A\). We
call \(A\) its \emph{source side}, \(B\) its \emph{target side}, and
\[
 p=\frac{\log(r_*/m_*)}{\log(B/A)}
\]
its \emph{pooled exponent}.
\end{definition}

In the bilinear reading of Section~\ref{subsec:prelim-conventions}, a
relative scheme computes \(m_*\) independent products of \(B\times B\)
matrices from \(r_*\) products of \(A\times A\) matrices whose inputs are
formed, and whose results are combined, by linear maps. Grouping the
\(r_*\) products created by one batch only among themselves would give the
exponent \((\log\lceil r_*/m_*\rceil)/\log(B/A)\); pooling all products
of one recursion depth removes the ceiling.

\begin{lemma}[Pooled recursion]\label{lem:batch}
Let \(r_*\MM_A\restr m_*\MM_B\) be a relative scheme with pooled exponent
\(p\). Then \(p\ge2\). The scheme yields a fixed arithmetic algorithm that
multiplies \(n\times n\) matrices with \(O(n^p)\) operations if \(p>2\),
and with \(O(n^2\log n)\) operations if \(p=2\); the implied constants
depend only on the scheme. Apart from multiplications by fixed
coefficients, every multiplication in this algorithm multiplies a linear
form in the entries of the left input matrix by a linear form in the
entries of the right input matrix.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The idea is to pool all products of one recursion depth, which have the
same side, and to cut them into batches of \(m_*\) regardless of which
batch created them; their number then grows by the factor \(r_*/m_*\) per
depth, up to one partly filled batch, while the side shrinks by the factor
\(A/B\).

\emph{Step 1: \(p\ge2\).} The flattening of \(\MM_A\) along leg \(1\) has
rank \(A^2\), because the rows of the variables \(x_{\alpha\beta}\) are
nonzero and have disjoint supports. Flattening ranks add over direct sums
and do not increase under restriction, so \(r_*A^2\ge m_*B^2\). Thus
\(r_*/m_*\ge(B/A)^2>1\), which gives \(p\ge2\) and \(r_*>m_*\).

\emph{Step 2: the recursion.} Put \(\rho=A/B<1\) and
\(n_\infty=A/(1-\rho)\), and call \(n_*=\lceil n_\infty\rceil+1\) the
\emph{leaf threshold}. At depth \(\ell\) the pooled recursion holds
\(\nu_\ell\) products of \(n_\ell\times n_\ell\) matrices, starting from
\(\nu_0=1\) and \(n_0=n\). If \(n_\ell\le n_*\), then \(\ell\) is the last
depth, denoted \(\ell_{\max}\), and its products are computed by the
classical algorithm. If \(n_\ell>n_*\), the products are cut into
\(\lceil\nu_\ell/m_*\rceil\) batches of \(m_*\), the last one filled up
with products of zero matrices, and each input matrix is padded with zeros
to side \(B\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil\) and cut into a \(B\times B\) array of
blocks of side \(\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil\). A batch is then an instance of
\(m_*\MM_B\) whose entries are blocks; the maps of the scheme on legs \(1\)
and \(2\) form from it the inputs of \(r_*\) products of side
\(A\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil\), and the map on leg \(3\) forms the \(m_*\)
outputs from their results. This is valid for blocks: the scheme is an
identity of trilinear forms with scalar coefficients in which each product
has a linear form in the left input before one in the right input, so it
holds over the noncommutative ring of block matrices. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pooled-recurrence}
 \nu_{\ell+1}=r_*\lceil\nu_\ell/m_*\rceil
 \le\frac{r_*}{m_*}\nu_\ell+r_*,
 \qquad
 n_{\ell+1}=A\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil .
\end{equation}
Apart from the products, the work at a depth \(\ell<\ell_{\max}\)
(padding, cutting into blocks, the fixed linear maps on at most
\(\nu_\ell\) batches of blocks of side \(\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil\le n_\ell\),
and removing the padding) takes \(O(\nu_\ell n_\ell^2)\) operations, since
\(B\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil<n_\ell+B\).

The computation runs in two passes: a downward pass forms the inputs and
records the batches depth by depth, the products at depth \(\ell_{\max}\)
are computed classically, and an upward pass applies the output maps depth
by depth, discarding the padding and the zero products. Inputs at depth
\(\ell+1\) depend only on inputs at depth \(\ell\), and outputs at
depth \(\ell\) only on outputs at depth \(\ell+1\), so products created
by different batches may share a batch.

\emph{Step 3: counts and depths.} Since \(r_*>m_*\), induction
on~\eqref{eq:pooled-recurrence} with the geometric sum
\(\sum_{\ell'<\ell}(r_*/m_*)^{\ell'}\le(r_*/m_*)^\ell m_*/(r_*-m_*)\) gives
\(\nu_\ell\le\bigl(1+\frac{r_*m_*}{r_*-m_*}\bigr)(r_*/m_*)^\ell\); as
\(r_*/m_*=\rho^{-p}\) by the definition of \(p\), this is
\(\nu_\ell=O(\rho^{-p\ell})\). For the sides,
\(n_\ell/B\le\lceil n_\ell/B\rceil<n_\ell/B+1\) gives
\(\rho n_\ell\le n_{\ell+1}<\rho n_\ell+A\), and since
\(A=(1-\rho)n_\infty\), the upper bound reads
\(n_{\ell+1}-n_\infty<\rho(n_\ell-n_\infty)\). Hence
\[
 \rho^\ell n\le n_\ell<n_\infty+\rho^\ell n .
\]
If depth \(\ell\) is not the last one, then \(n_\ell>n_*\ge n_\infty+1\),
so \(\rho^\ell n>n_\ell-n_\infty>1\); as \(\rho^\ell n\to0\), the
recursion stops. Taking \(\ell=\ell_{\max}-1\) gives
\(\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n>\rho\), which also holds for \(\ell_{\max}=0\)
because \(n\ge1>\rho\); and \(\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n\le n_{\ell_{\max}}\le
n_*\). Thus
\[
 \rho<\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n\le n_*,\qquad\text{so}\qquad
 \ell_{\max}=\log_{1/\rho}n+O(1).
\]

\emph{Step 4: total cost.} Each classical product at the last depth costs
at most \(2n_*^3\) operations, so the leaves cost
\(O(\nu_{\ell_{\max}})=O(\rho^{-p\ell_{\max}})\). By Steps 2 and 3, and
since \(n_\ell^2\le2n_\infty^2+2\rho^{2\ell}n^2\), the work at a depth
\(\ell<\ell_{\max}\) is \(O(\rho^{-p\ell}+\rho^{(2-p)\ell}n^2)\).
Including the last depth, the total cost is therefore
\[
 O\Bigl(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\ell_{\max}}
 \bigl(\rho^{-p\ell}+\rho^{(2-p)\ell}n^2\bigr)\Bigr).
\]
The first sum is geometric with ratio \(\rho^{-p}>1\), hence
\(O(\rho^{-p\ell_{\max}})=O\bigl(n^p(\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n)^{-p}\bigr)\). If
\(p>2\), the second sum is geometric with ratio \(\rho^{2-p}>1\), hence
\(O(\rho^{(2-p)\ell_{\max}}n^2)=O\bigl(n^p(\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n)^{2-p}\bigr)\).
Both are \(O(n^p)\) because \(\rho^{\ell_{\max}}n>\rho\). If \(p=2\), each
of the \(\ell_{\max}+1=O(\log n)\) terms of the second sum equals \(n^2\).

\emph{Step 5: bilinearity.} Padding, cutting into blocks, batching, and the
maps of the scheme on legs \(1\) and \(2\) act linearly and separately on
left and right inputs, so by induction on the depth every entry of a left
(right) input at every depth is a linear form in the entries of the
original left (right) input. The classical algorithm at the last depth
multiplies an entry of a left input by an entry of a right input, and all
other multiplications are by fixed coefficients.
\end{proof}
